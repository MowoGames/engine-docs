A level the size of the Intel Sponza (2022, with the Curtains and Ivy add-ons: about 10.8 million triangles in 450 submeshes and 80 4K PBR textures) runs on the same renderer as a small one. On a mid-range GPU its frame time is decided by the [quality tier](/docs/rendering/quality-tiers/) and the resolution far more than by the size of the meshes. This page shows where the time goes and what the engine does so the geometry itself stays cheap.

## What the engine does with a big model

- **Static meshes are drawn by the GPU.** Every static, opaque mesh joins the GPU scene: its vertices and indices are copied once into a shared geometry pool, the GPU culls the instances against the frustum and the previous frame's depth, and all meshes that share a material draw with one indirect call. The pools grow with the level, a pool at a time, so a large level stays on this path. Their total size is capped by `r.GpuScene.GeometryPoolBudgetMB` (by default an eighth of the GPU's memory, between 256 and 2048 MB); a mesh that no longer fits is drawn on its own and the log says so once.
- **Textures are block-compressed.** An imported texture reaches the GPU as a BC chain with all its mips — BC7 for colour, BC5 for normal maps, BC6H for HDR — as the packaged game ships it. A 4K map takes 21 MB instead of the 85 MB it took uncompressed; on the Sponza that is the difference between 8.5 GB of loaded assets, past what an 8 GB card holds, and a level that fits. An author who sets a texture's compression to none keeps it uncompressed.
- **The first open cooks, later opens read the cache.** The first time the editor opens a level it compresses every texture into the project's [Derived Data Cache](/docs/content-pipeline/derived-data-cache/) on all CPU cores, in the background: the level is usable at once, untextured surfaces fill in as their maps land, and the status bar counts the assets still importing. On the reference machine the Sponza's 80 textures cook in about a minute; every later open reads them from the cache.
- **Entering Play does not stall on the level's size.** The sound occlusion and the scanner build their own copy of the level's triangles on a worker thread.

## Where the milliseconds go

The Sponza in Play at 1920×1080, the level's cinematic camera flying through the nave, Global Illumination on, measured on the reference RTX 3050 (8 GB) with a development build, nothing else compiling. *Auto* is the Auto upscaling mode (`r.Upscaler.AutoQuality 1`, Quality at 1080p), *native* renders every pixel.

| Tier | Auto | Native | GPU frame (Auto) | Largest passes (Auto, ms) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low | 250 fps | 147 fps | 4.4 ms | G-buffer 1.3, post-processing 0.8, transparency 0.6 |
| Medium | 110 fps | 55 fps | 9.6 ms | GI sampling 1.8, post-processing 1.6, GI denoise 1.4, upscale 1.3 |
| High | 78 fps | 44 fps | 13.2 ms | GI sampling 3.3, shadows 2.3, transparency 1.4, GI denoise 1.4, SSR 1.4, G-buffer 1.1 |
| High, GI off | 121 fps | 70 fps | 8.6 ms | shadows 1.8, G-buffer 1.7, SSR 1.3 |
| Epic | 32 fps | 16 fps* | 31.6 ms | GI sampling 8.2, shadows 5.0, ray-traced GI gather 4.0, reflections 4.2 (screen trace 0.5, ray-traced world leg 2.8) |
| Cinematic | 19 fps | 11 fps | 44.9 ms | GI sampling 7.9, SSR 7.1, volumetric fog 6.1, ray-traced sun shadow 6.0, GTAO 5.1, RTAO 4.5 |
| High at 2560×1440 | 51 fps | 23 fps | 19.8 ms | GI sampling 4.9, shadows 3.9, SSR 2.2 |

\* Measured before Epic traced its screen-space reflections at half resolution, as High does: that cut the reflection passes from 7.9 to 4.1 ms (Auto 27-30 fps before, 32 after) with an image inside the noise of two full-resolution captures of the same view.

Drawing the geometry — the G-buffer pass with its culling — costs about 1 ms of a High frame. The meshlet G-buffer (Epic and up) did not lower it on the Sponza: forced on at High it drew the level in the same 1.2 ms, but at that time its cluster pool held only 38 of the level's 446 meshes, so High keeps the classic indirect draws (see [Very dense meshes on Epic](#very-dense-meshes-on-epic)). The rest is lighting: Global Illumination, shadows and screen-space reflections, all of which scale with the number of pixels and with the tier. So:

- **Epic and Cinematic are not for a mid-range GPU.** They add ray-traced and SDF features whose cost on a 3050 is three to four times High's whole frame. On such a card use High or Medium.
- **Render fewer pixels.** Auto upscaling roughly doubles the frame rate at every tier for little visible loss; native rendering on a mid-range GPU is the most expensive single choice after the tier.
- **Global Illumination is the largest single cost on High** (about 4.6 ms of 13.2). A project that does not need dynamic indirect light can turn it off in Project Settings.

## Very dense meshes on Epic

On Epic and Cinematic, static meshes with an import-built cluster hierarchy draw through the meshlet G-buffer: the GPU picks, per cluster, the coarsest level whose error stays under a pixel and culls clusters against the frustum and the previous frame's depth. Two things decide whether a large level gets that benefit:

- **The cluster pool grows with the level.** It starts at about 63 MB. When it is full of meshes that are all still on screen, the arena that ran short doubles at the start of the next frame and the meshes come back with their page layouts. It never grows past `r.Meshlet.PoolBudgetMB` (by default a sixteenth of the GPU's memory) and never into the last tenth of the GPU's memory budget, so a level that already fills the card keeps the classic path for what does not fit instead of running out of memory. Before, the pool stopped at 65 536 clusters; the Intel Sponza fit 38 of its 446 meshes.
- **Page layouts are built on worker threads.** The first frame that draws a mesh used to build its page layout on the render thread; the mesh now draws through the classic path for the frame or two until a worker has it ready.

A synthetic field of 32 unique rocks (4.7 million unique triangles, 18.9 million submitted, the `huge-scene-test` row) at 1920×1080 on the reference RTX 3050, development build, nothing compiling:

| | GPU frame | G-buffer | Shadows | Worst CPU frame while meshes arrive |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Classic indirect draws | 28.9 ms | 8.6 ms | 17.7 ms | — |
| Meshlet, pool fixed at 63 MB (18 of 32 rocks) | 9.0 ms | 3.5 ms | 2.7 ms | — |
| Meshlet, pool grown to 128 MB (all rocks) | 5.5 ms | 1.1 ms | 0.8 ms | 24 ms (202 ms with layouts built on the render thread) |

On Vulkan the same field takes 30.2 ms classic and 8.1 ms on the grown pool. The pool's growth cost 66 MB of VRAM here.

The Sponza on Epic is the opposite case: on an 8 GB card it already uses about 98 % of the VRAM budget, so the pool stops growing at 124 MB and the rest of the level keeps the classic path. Letting it grow to 328 MB there took the frame rate from 24 to 9 fps, which is why growth reads the live budget.

A pool that is full and still asked for more (a big level, a fast camera) evicts and admits meshes every frame. Before November 2026 that could shred meshes: each page-in's vertex gather read its page table from an upload buffer that the next eviction, or the next frame, had already rewritten, so the gather filled the wrong slices. Every frame now writes its own tables, and the `meshlet-pool-churn-test` row reads the whole pool back while groups of meshes churn through it on D3D12 and Vulkan: 1.2 million of 1.8 million checked vertices were wrong before the fix, none after.

The pool ages a mesh by frames the GPU has finished with, not by views drawn. A renderer that draws several views in one frame (a viewport with a scene capture, a split view) used to count each view as a frame, so a mesh's slices came back for reuse after a single GPU frame while the previous frame was still reading them. Eviction and the retire queue now follow the device's frame counter; the `meshlet-pool-churn-test` row runs its churn again with three views per frame and no idle between frames.

### Cluster pages from disk: not yet

The cluster hierarchy of every mesh still lives in system memory once the mesh is loaded (179 MB for the Sponza), and the pool pages from that copy. Paging it from the cooked package is designed (the engine's internal notes hold the design) and not shipped: it needs a cook chunk of page-ordered payloads, a page source behind the pool, and asynchronous reads with a byte budget per frame. A package cooked today keeps working unchanged when it lands, because the new chunk is optional and the pool builds a layout at run time for a package without it. It was not built because no measured level needs it yet: the Sponza's cluster data is 2 % of the editor's working set on Epic, and the pool, not the system copy, is what limits large levels on an 8 GB card.

## Check your own settings

The editor keeps the tier and upscaling mode you pick in your user settings, and they apply to every project you open. If a level runs far slower for you than the table above, check the **Quality** preset and the upscaling mode you saved: Epic with native rendering costs a 3050 about five times what High with Auto costs. On a GPU with less than 10 GB of memory the Quality card and **Project Settings > Scalability** say so under Epic and Cinematic, with the rate the reference card measured.

To see the numbers for your own level, enable `stat.FrameStats 300` in the console: every 300 frames the log lists the CPU frame, the GPU frame and every GPU pass with its time.

## Settings

| Console variable | Default | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `r.GpuScene.GeometryPoolGrowth` | 1 | 1 adds a geometry pool when a mesh does not fit; 0 keeps one fixed pool per index width and draws the rest one by one (for comparison) |
| `r.GpuScene.GeometryPoolBudgetMB` | 0 | Total size of the geometry pools in MB; 0 = an eighth of the GPU's dedicated memory, 256 to 2048 MB |
| `r.Texture.PackageNativeBc` | 1 | Upload a texture as its block-compressed chain; 0 decodes it and compresses it while it loads |
| `r.Meshlet.PoolBudgetMB` | 0 | Most VRAM the meshlet cluster pool may grow to, in MB; 0 = a sixteenth of the GPU's dedicated memory, 64 to 1024 MB; 64 or less keeps the starting pool. Growth also stops short of the last tenth of the GPU's memory budget |
| `r.Meshlet.AsyncLayouts` | 1 | Build a mesh's meshlet page layout on a worker thread the first time it is drawn; 0 builds it on the render thread (for comparison) |
| `r.MeshletResidencyStats` | 0 | Every N frames log the meshlet pool's residency, capacity, rejections and the system memory the meshes' cluster hierarchies hold |
